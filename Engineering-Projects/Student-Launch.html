NASA Student Launch

Whats Nasa Student Launch?

The NASA Student Launch Competition is an annual event that challenges student teams to design, construct, and launch high-powered rockets aimed at reaching specified altitude and payload objectives. This competition emphasizes the development of STEM skills by immersing participants in real-world aerospace engineering projects, including everything from design documentation to actual flight testing. It culminates in a launch at NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center, providing invaluable hands-on experience in both engineering and collaboration. 

Our university earned a place in the competition after securing a win at the NASA First Nations Launch (FNL). We were tasked with the design and manufacture of a high power rocket reaching a target altitude between 4,000-5000 ft. carrying a payload which featured an automated camera system capable of imaging the immediate landing vicinity.

Avionics

The avionics and recovery system for our high-power rocket was carefully engineered to ensure accurate data capture, controlled descent, and successful recovery. The avionics subsystem featured dual Featherweight Raven altimeters (Raven 3 and Raven 4), providing redundancy for detecting key flight events such as apogee and parachute deployment. Each altimeter was powered by an independent LiPo battery, reducing the risk of power failure. These altimeters triggered black powder charges housed in custom ejection canisters, optimized through ground testing to provide consistent deployment force. The recovery system used a dual-deployment strategy, starting with an 18-inch Fruity Chutes drogue parachute at apogee to stabilize the rocket and maintain a descent speed of around 70 mph. At 600 feet AGL, a 72-inch Iris main parachute deployed, slowing the final descent to about 20 mph and ensuring a safe landing within the mission’s 90-second descent time requirement.

To enable real-time tracking and efficient recovery, the system included a Featherweight GPS unit operating on a 915 MHz frequency, transmitting continuous location data to a ground station. As a member of the avionics team, I was responsible for the GPS system’s functionality and integration. This involved securing the GPS unit within the avionics bay, ensuring consistent signal transmission, and conducting pre-flight configuration and testing. I also verified telemetry data during flight tests and post-flight analysis, ensuring accurate tracking and efficient rocket recovery. This robust system contributed to a 100% recovery success rate over five test flights, demonstrating the design’s reliability and effectiveness.

Stage Ejection

For the rocket’s dual-deployment system, two black powder charges are required—one to shear the pins holding the aft section with the main parachute and another for the forward section housing the drogue parachute. Previously, we used custom PVC blast caps, but these had become damaged and unusable. In response, we engineered a reliable alternative by constructing pressure vessels from nitrile gloves, containing the black powder and securing the assembly with electrical tape to build sufficient pressure. Determining the correct black powder charge proved challenging. To establish a starting point, I calculated an estimate based upon the ideal gas law. From there, we conducted eight incremental ground tests at 0.25g intervals for both the forward and aft sections until we achieved optimal separation.
